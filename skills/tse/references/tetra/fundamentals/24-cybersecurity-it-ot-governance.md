# Cybersecurity, IT/OT Convergence & Governance

The network-security layer that sits alongside (not instead of) the air-interface/E2E encryption covered in the security fundamentals file (10) — read this when the question is "how do we keep the TETRA core's IP network itself secure," not "how do we encrypt a call."

## Why this is a separate concern from air-interface/E2E encryption

Fundamentals file 10 covers protecting radio traffic in transit. This file covers a different attack surface entirely: the TETRA exchange/core and its NMS (fundamentals file 17) increasingly run on standard IP networking, meaning the same categories of cyber risk that apply to any industrial IP network — unauthorised access, malware, lateral movement from a compromised IT system — apply to the TETRA core too, independent of whether radio traffic itself is encrypted. A TETRA network with excellent air-interface encryption but a core network directly reachable from the client's general corporate IT network has a real exposure that the encryption alone does nothing to close.

## IT/OT segmentation — the core principle

**IT/OT convergence** describes the general industry trend of operational technology (OT — the systems that directly run physical operations: SCADA, safety systems, and here, the mission-critical radio network) increasingly sharing IP infrastructure with conventional IT (corporate email, business applications). The standard mitigation, and the one most O&G/rail cybersecurity policies now mandate, is **strict network segmentation**: the TETRA core sits in its own isolated network zone (a dedicated VLAN/subnet behind a firewall, ideally following a recognised segmentation model such as the Purdue model used widely in industrial cybersecurity), with tightly controlled, explicitly-justified pathways to anything outside that zone — never a flat network where the TETRA core is reachable the same way a corporate laptop is.

## IEC 62443 and NIS2 — what they actually require

- **IEC 62443** — the internationally recognised standard series for industrial automation and control systems (IACS) cybersecurity, directly applicable to a TETRA core treated as OT infrastructure. It defines security levels (SL 1–4, increasing rigor) and a structured process for zone/conduit segmentation (the "zones and conduits" model — essentially the formal version of the segmentation principle above), plus requirements spanning access control, patch management, and security lifecycle management for both the asset owner and the system integrator/vendor. A project citing IEC 62443 compliance should specify which parts of the series and which target security level apply — "IEC 62443 compliant" without that specificity isn't a verifiable requirement.
- **NIS2** — the EU's updated Network and Information Security directive, imposing cybersecurity risk-management and incident-reporting obligations on operators of essential/important services (which explicitly includes rail transport operators, and can include O&G depending on jurisdiction and scale) — a **regulatory** obligation on the asset owner, not a technical standard for the equipment itself, but one that shapes what the technical design needs to support (incident detection/logging capability, defined incident-reporting timelines) so the operator can actually meet its own NIS2 obligations. Confirm applicability with the client's own compliance function — NIS2 is EU-jurisdiction-specific, and outside the EU an equivalent national critical-infrastructure cybersecurity regulation may apply instead, requiring its own confirmation.

## RBAC and audit logging — the administrative-access control layer

Separate again from both air-interface encryption and network segmentation: **who is allowed to administer the TETRA core itself** (provision new subscribers, change talkgroup configuration, apply firmware patches) is its own access-control surface. **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)** restricts each administrator to the specific functions their role actually requires (a subscriber-provisioning operator shouldn't necessarily have firmware-patching rights, and vice versa) rather than a flat "administrator" role with unrestricted access — standard practice for any system with security/safety consequences, and specifically expected by both IEC 62443's access-control requirements and most rail/O&G internal cybersecurity policy. **Immutable audit logging** — a tamper-evident record of every administrative action (who did what, when) — is the accompanying control that makes RBAC meaningful in practice: without an audit trail, a misconfiguration or malicious change can't be reliably traced back to its source. Both RBAC granularity and audit-log retention/immutability should be confirmed as explicit OEM platform capabilities during Stage 2 evaluation, not assumed present by default — the granularity of role definitions and the tamper-resistance of the log storage are genuinely OEM-specific.

## Why this matters for design decisions

- Network segmentation (TETRA core isolated from general corporate IT) should be an explicit architecture requirement in the technical specification, ideally naming the segmentation model/security level being targeted (e.g. IEC 62443 SL-2), not left as an assumed best practice.
- If the client is subject to NIS2 or an equivalent critical-infrastructure cybersecurity regulation, confirm what incident-detection and reporting capability the technical design needs to support to help the client meet that obligation — this is a client compliance question to raise early, not something the contractor can resolve alone.
- RBAC structure and audit-log immutability are OEM platform capabilities to confirm explicitly during vendor evaluation — treat "the platform has security features" as insufficient without specifying what RBAC granularity and log retention/tamper-resistance are actually available.

Formal reference: IEC 62443 series (industrial automation/control systems security); EU NIS2 Directive (2022/2555) and equivalent national critical-infrastructure cybersecurity regulation outside the EU — always confirm current applicability and specific clauses against the project's jurisdiction, since this is a fast-moving regulatory area.
